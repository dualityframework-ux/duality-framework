Case study

Automating the weekly variance write-up

How I turned the most repetitive part of inventory control, explaining why the count was off, into a tool that does the first pass.

Ian KachadorianOctober 20266 min read

The problem

Every week I research more than 150 inventory discrepancies in a distribution center, averaging around $30,000 in gross adjustments. Any variance over $1,000 needs a written root cause in the weekly audit response.

Most of that time is not detective work. It is recognizing patterns I have seen hundreds of times:

  • The same part is short 12 in one slot and over 12 in the slot next to it.
  • A box of 12 was counted as one.
  • Someone keyed 64 when the shelf held 46.
  • Two headlight bulbs that look identical are each off by the same amount, in opposite directions.

Pattern recognition that a person does the same way every week is exactly what a tool should do first. The person's time should go to the variances that do not fit a pattern.

The approach

I built Variance around the same four steps I follow by hand.

1. Measure both ways

Gross accuracy counts every dollar of variance. Net accuracy lets overages cancel shortages. Reporting only net hides problems, so Variance shows both.

A 99% net looks clean. The gross number shows more than $11,000 of stock sitting in the wrong place or recorded wrong.

2. Prioritize by dollars

A Pareto view ranks every line by absolute dollar impact. In the sample, 9 of 28 variance lines account for 80% of the gross. Those get looked at first.

3. Classify the likely cause

Each variance is checked against eight causes, in a deliberate order. The order matters because one real problem can look like two different ones:

OrderCauseWhy it comes here
1Mislocated stockA battery short at C-14-02 and over at C-15-04 is one move, not an empty slot and a found slot. Checking pairs first prevents two wrong write-ups.
2Unit of measureAn exact pack multiple, like 300 against 25, is unambiguous. Requiring a difference of at least 5 units keeps small miscounts out.
3Transposed digitsSame digits, different order. Only checked for two-digit quantities and up.
4Look-alike swapTwo different SKUs in the same aisle, off by about the same amount in opposite directions.
5TimingMovement within a few days of the count, so a transaction may not have posted.
6–7Empty and foundOnly after the pairing checks, so they are not mistaken for halves of a move.
8Counter accuracyLast on purpose. Blaming the count is a judgment about a person, so it applies only to lines nothing else explains, and only for a counter whose variance rate is well above the team's.

4. Draft the explanation

For every line over the threshold, Variance writes a first draft in the same structure I use for every analysis: observation, mechanism, implication.

Observation. BAT-5100 (Battery, group 24F) at C-14-02: system 18, counted 6, a variance of −12 units (−$1,704.00).

Mechanism. The same item is short in one location and over by about the same amount in another, which points to a put-away or replenishment move that went to the wrong slot. The offsetting variance is at C-15-04.

Implication. Move the stock back to its home location and correct both locations in one transfer, not two adjustments. Review the put-away that touched both slots.

The result

I tested it on a synthetic count of 164 lines with 14 problems planted in it: two mislocations, two unit-of-measure errors, a transposition, a look-alike swap, two timing issues, two empty locations, a found location, and one counter with a high error rate.

  • All 14 were classified correctly, and the counter was flagged. An automated test checks this on every change.
  • It also found something I did not plant: a serpentine belt over 3 in one aisle and short 3 in another. That came from the random noise in the sample, and it is exactly what a real offsetting pair looks like. Whether a 3-unit pair is worth a transfer is a judgment call, which is the point: the tool surfaces it and a person decides.
  • The four lines over $1,000 come with drafted write-ups ready to verify and paste.

The limits

  • These are rules, not a model. The tolerances (pairs within 10–15%, swaps in the same aisle) are judgment calls from experience. Different warehouses would want different settings.
  • It only sees the count. Without transaction history it can say a variance looks like a mislocation, not prove it. Every draft is a starting point to verify.
  • Timing needs data. The timing check only works if the export includes a last-movement date.
  • It has only seen synthetic data. No employer data was used to build or test it.

What I would build next

  1. Pull transaction history to confirm a cause instead of inferring it.
  2. Learn the tolerances from write-ups a person has confirmed, so the rules fit each building.
  3. Generate the recount list automatically: which locations to recount, in walking order.

Built with plain JavaScript and no dependencies. The analysis logic lives in one file of pure functions, tested in Node and run on every push with GitHub Actions. Files never leave the browser.
