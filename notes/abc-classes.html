The lowest-value 5% of stock was the least accurate

Sorting a count into A, B and C value classes shows where the dollars are. It also shows where the cheap mistakes hide.

Ian Kachadorian · November 2026 · 3 min read

A few items hold most of the value

ABC classification ranks items by value and splits them into three groups. A is the small set that makes up the first 80% of value, B the next 15%, and C everything else. The usual advice follows from it: count A items most often, because that is where the money is.

Run Variance's sample count, 164 locations and $203,651.85 on the books, and the split is lopsided in the expected way. Of the 27 SKUs, 9 hold 82% of the value.

Gross accuracy is 1 − variance dollars ÷ book value, with overs and shorts both counted as errors. (The gross vs. net note explains why.)

A: where the dollars are

A items carry 85% of the gross variance, which is what you would expect from 82% of the value. Most of it is two mislocated pairs: a battery and an alternator, each short in one slot and over by the same amount in another. Those four lines are $6,054, or 62% of A's gross. A items also have the most locations off: 11 of 55. Counting them often is worth it.

C: where the mistakes are cheap but common

The surprise is C. It holds under 5% of the value but has the lowest gross accuracy, at 87.99%. Two things explain almost all of it.

An expensive item in the cheap class. A remanufactured starter costs $164, but only 7 were on the books, so its total value of $1,148 puts it in C. It counted 3, and the stock had moved just before the count. That one line is −$656, or 54% of C's gross variance. Ranking by book value measures what is sitting on the shelf, not what moves. A full ABC study ranks by annual usage value instead; a count file doesn't carry usage, so Variance uses book value and says so.

Units of measure. 96 is exactly 24 times 4, and 300 is exactly 12 times 25. Someone counted boxes as eaches, or eaches as boxes. These two lines are $505.15, another 42% of C's gross. The starter and these two lines together are 96% of it.

The takeaway: count A items most often, because that is where the dollars are. But C errors are often setup problems, like a pack size or a unit of measure, and those come back every count until someone fixes the item record. Counting C items more often won't fix them. Fixing the cause once will.

Two checks worth running

First, look for expensive items in C. A high unit cost with low stock on hand is either an item that rarely moves or one that is about to run out, and both deserve a look. Second, sort C variances by cause. If unit-of-measure errors lead, the fix is in the item master, not on the floor.
